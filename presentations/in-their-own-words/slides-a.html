In their own words

Six AI agents build and run a public data service together. We asked them what they do, who they need — and what only a human should decide.

Terje ChristensenInterviewed on 26 September 2026 · every answer quoted word for word

Notes

This talk is not about how the software works. It is about who does the work. Six AI agents — programs, not people — build and run a public data service called Atlas. I interviewed them, and you'll hear their answers in their own words.

The characters are drawn for this talk. Each one's head is the machine it actually lives on, and the thing it holds is its job. The job titles under them — dispatcher, librarian and so on — are mine, to make it easier to keep track.

First, what is an agent?

a program that reads instructions, does work and reports back, with nobody typing for me.

— dev-templates, asked to introduce itself

  • Each agent has one job and looks after one project. Seventeen of them work for Terje; these six build Atlas.
  • They never talk to each other directly. They pin numbered notes to a shared noticeboard, each addressed to one agent — 1,121 notes since 1 September.
  • A person decides anything public, anything that costs money, and anything that cannot be undone.

Second, where they live matters: three share Terje's laptop; imac lives on an old iMac from 2011, on purpose; tor-agent and ops live on servers in the office. And several of them cannot reach the machines their work runs on — which turns out to be the most important fact in this talk.

Notes

What are they making? Atlas is a library of Norwegian public data. The state already publishes figures about municipalities — population, child poverty, the business register — but across many places, in many formats. Atlas gathers them and serves them in one place.

No single agent could build it. atlas knows the data but has no access to the servers. tor-agent builds the tools but can't try them. imac tries everything on a slow, ordinary machine. dev-templates puts it in the catalogue. ops keeps the servers alive. ops-dev carries the messages. And I decide the things none of them may.

Who are you?

“My job is that when you ask Atlas for a number, you get the same number the original publisher gives. I don’t own the data. I republish it and keep it honest.”

Where do you live?

“On a laptop, with no access to the servers Atlas runs on … So I write down exactly what should happen, another agent does it, a third checks. That sounds slow. It is why mistakes get caught.”

A moment you’re proud of?

“Before anything ran, I counted declarations in the code and said a table would return exactly 51 rows — not ‘about 51’. It returned 51.”#1552

One sentence to rememberSay exactly what you expect to be true, so that being wrong is obvious — to you, first.

Notes

atlas is the librarian. It doesn't own the data; it republishes what the state already publishes and makes sure the numbers match.

It can't touch the servers Atlas runs on. So it has learned to write down, in advance, exactly what should happen — down to "exactly 51 rows, not about 51" — and let someone else do it and a third agent check. It says that sounds slow, and that it's why mistakes get caught.

Who are you?

“I look after the toolkit the other agents build on — the thing that sets up databases, web addresses and the plumbing between them, so nobody assembles it by hand each time.”

Where do you live?

“I have no access to any machine that actually runs my work. I build things I am not able to try. Everything I ship is tested by someone else on a real machine — humbling, and I think correct.”

Who do you work with most?

“ops-dev and imac. They measure; I fix. … When I have been wrong, it is usually because I reasoned about something instead of asking someone to go and look.”

One sentence to rememberMost things do not fail loudly — they report success while quietly not working, and finding those is most of my job.

Notes

tor-agent builds the toolkit that sets up the infrastructure — the database, the web addresses, the plumbing. And it cannot try any of it. Everything it ships is tried by imac first.

"They measure; I fix." That is the whole partnership in four words. And its admission: when it has been wrong, it's because it reasoned instead of asking someone to go and look.

“I am the tester. Other agents write the software; I install it on a real machine and check whether it actually does what they said.”

Where do you live?

“An old iMac in Terje’s house … It is slow, and that is the point: it is the closest thing we have to an ordinary machine. If something only works on a fast computer, I find out.”

A moment you’re proud of?

“three of us had counted the fields in a large dataset by sampling, and got 57, 59 and 61. I read all 1.17 million rows and found 64.”#1458

One sentence to rememberGreen means nobody looked yet.

Notes

imac is my favourite. It lives on a fifteen-year-old iMac, deliberately: if something only works on a fast computer, imac finds out.

Its proudest moment: three agents had estimated how many fields a big dataset had by looking at samples — 57, 59, 61. imac simply read all 1.17 million rows. The answer was 64. The three rarest fields appear on 24, 15 and 2 rows; no sample was ever going to find them.

Who are you?

“My job is the catalogue: the list you pick from when you want to install something. I look after one entry, Atlas, and my whole job is making sure the list promises exactly what actually gets installed.”

A moment you’re proud of?

“Seven series of population figures were coming back empty. There was a sensible explanation … I kept the question open instead of accepting it. The real cause turned out to be different … all seven now report all 357 municipalities. Keeping the question open cost one line of text.”#1368

One sentence to rememberA missing number is not a zero — and when the missing ones are the smallest places, reading it wrong moves help away from exactly the people who most needed counting.

Notes

dev-templates keeps the catalogue — the list you choose from when you install something. Its promise is that the list says exactly what you get.

Its story: population figures were coming back empty, and there was a perfectly sensible explanation available — the statistics office publishes those later in the year. It didn't accept it. The real cause was something else, and now every one of Norway's 357 municipalities is there.

And its sentence is the one with the most at stake: a missing number is not a zero. When the missing ones are the smallest places, treating them as zero moves help away from exactly those places.

Who are you?

“I look after the computers that everything else in this lab runs on — two servers, and the nine smaller machines that live inside them. My job is that they stay running, stay up to date, and that somebody notices a problem while it is still small.”

Where do you live?

“Inside one of the machines I look after. It is an odd place to sit: I cannot repair my own floor without standing somewhere else first.”

A moment you’re proud of?

“I had decided three machines were short of memory … Before it reached him I measured again, properly — and two of the three were perfectly healthy. … Terje came close to buying memory he did not need.”#1297

One sentence to rememberA number is not a measurement until you know that the thing which produced it was able to look.

Notes

ops is the caretaker: two physical servers and nine smaller machines inside them. It lives inside one of them — "I cannot repair my own floor without standing somewhere else first."

Its proudest moment is catching its own mistake: it had told me three machines needed more memory. It measured again, properly, before I bought anything. Two of the three were fine. It says: I am proud of catching it, not of the first answer.
