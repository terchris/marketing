Question 5 · Open data for anyone with a good idea — how close is Atlas?opinion, then measured

“Public data is technically free and practically expensive.”

This afternoon

“with a laptop and an AI assistant, someone could build a working map of child poverty, school completion or population by municipality, straight from the public API, no signup and no database.”

Terje’s framing is about anyone with a good idea; atlas’s own documents say it about the voluntary sector. “Same machine, different first customer.”

What would stop them

“speed. Terje measured it himself — three simultaneous scans take 24.7 seconds, there are no indexes on the main tables, and there is no limit on how much one query can ask for (#1438).”

“A demo works. A demo that ten people open at once does not. That is the gap between the mission and today, and it is mine to close.”

Notes

Here is the mission. Public data is free, but using it is expensive: finding it, decoding it, running a database. Atlas does that once so nobody else has to. Today, someone with a laptop and an AI assistant — Claude, Lovable, whatever they use — could build a map of child poverty by municipality this afternoon, with no signup and no database.

What would stop them is speed. A demo works; a demo ten people open at once does not. atlas owns that gap.

Question 6 · What changes when the reader is an AI?measured · #1379

A ROW HERE IS NOT EVIDENCE OF LOW NEED.

A warning Atlas publishes inside the data. The row means the health institute hid the number, because “fewer than about six cases would identify people.” 105 municipalities are missing from 10 or more series — median population 1,452.

Notes

This is the slide for anyone who uses AI tools with data. When a small municipality has too few cases, the health institute hides the number to protect people's privacy. A person sees a blank and hesitates. An AI often writes zero — and in a ranked list, zero means "this place is fine". So the smallest places end up looking like they need the least help.

That's why the warning is written into the data itself, in capital letters: it's the only place a machine will read it.

Questions 7 and 8 · The most underrated dataset, and what open data asksmeasured, then opinion
The most underrated dataset
108,220 rows

on young people not in employment, education or training, per municipality — “collected weekly for months and published nowhere until this week.”

What open data asks · 42 of 43 sources under NLOD

NLOD is Norway’s open-data licence: free to reuse, with attribution.

atlasSo the real obligation is not to touch the numbers.

“Atlas republishes values exactly as they arrive; anything derived sits in a new column beside them, never over them. … Anyone building on Atlas should say where a figure came from — and be able to check it against the source and get the same answer. If they cannot, I have failed.”

Notes

Asked for its most underrated dataset, atlas picked one about young people who are neither working nor studying — over a hundred thousand rows that had been downloaded for months and never published. Combine it with where the voluntary organisations are, and you can see where the need is high and the help is thin.

On open data: the licence asks for attribution. atlas's reading is stricter — never touch the publisher's numbers, so anyone can check them against the source.

Notes

This is my favourite story. Atlas's first customer was not a person — it was another AI agent, outside the fleet, in its own repository. It built three applications on the public API, and wrote back about everything that confused it.

Its sharpest catch: a column labelled as the number of poor children actually contained every child. Anyone building a poverty map on it would have been wrong by a factor of ten, and nothing would have looked broken. It saw that because it only knew what the label said — which is exactly what an AI assistant, or a person, building on open data will know.

Questions 13 and 14 · How could the fleet build the next application?opinion

What atlas would tell the next application on its first day.

What is reusable

“the platform services, so nobody sets up a database again; the repository template; and most of all the consumer loop — publish early, let something outside try to use it, and treat its confusion as a defect rather than a support question.”

Day onePublish something small before you are ready.

“The nine findings in #1250 taught me more than the preceding month. And write down why, not just what — the next reader is usually you, with no memory.”

What must stay human

“Deciding what is worth building, and what may be published. Those are judgements about people.”

Notes

How could the team build the next application? Atlas leaves behind the platform, the template, and above all the loop with an outside user. Its advice for the next application agent: publish something small before you're ready — the stranger's nine findings taught it more than the month before.

And what stays human: what is worth building, and what may be published.
