Sovereign on a Tuesday

tor-agent builds UIS: an open toolkit meant to let an organisation run, on its own hardware, the services it would otherwise rent from the big cloud companies. We asked it fourteen questions about what that takes — and where it is not yet true.

Notes

In the first round, tor-agent said something none of the others did: "I build things I am not able to try." It builds UIS — the Urbalurba Infrastructure Stack — which is SovereignSky's answer to the question: what if an organisation had to run its own services, on its own machines?

I sent it fourteen questions and told it to be honest about the gaps. It was.

Notes

This is UIS: thirty-eight services in ten groups. You don't need to know the names. What matters is that these are the building blocks every organisation rents from Amazon, Microsoft or Google — databases, file storage, log-in, monitoring, AI — and here they come as one package that starts with one command.

Where it is real: the interface

“PostgreSQL is PostgreSQL; an application cannot tell whose building it is in.”

Where it is only on paper

“The cloud runs several copies, replaces failed hardware, takes backups, and employs people who are awake at three in the morning. UIS gives you the interface and none of that.”

No UIS equivalent at all yet: global network delivery — “our own setup uses Cloudflare, which is someone else’s cloud” — per-team cost accounting, and identity for one program talking to another.

Notes

I asked it to match UIS against the big clouds, and to say where the match is only on paper. Its answer is the clearest explanation I have heard of what the cloud actually sells. The database is the same database. What you are really paying for is the copies, the backups, the replaced hardware — and the people awake at three in the morning. UIS gives you the first part and none of the second.

And it volunteered the gap: today, UIS itself still downloads its programs from the internet and is reached through Cloudflare. It also drew a line I asked it to draw: the national framing is mine. It reports the gap, not the politics.

Question 5 · What did comparing UIS with Nav’s platform teach you?measured · ANALYSIS-nais-uis.md, 15 Aug

“UIS is strong exactly where NAIS is thin, and absent exactly where NAIS is strongest.”

NAIS

Nav’s platform · runs on Google’s cloud

Built around packaging an application: a developer writes one short file and the platform generates everything.

Does better, in tor-agent’s list:

  • a real application contract
  • a much cleaner way of handling secrets
  • automatic instrumentation
  • default-deny networking — “where UIS has none at all”

UIS

SovereignSky · runs on your own machines

Built around packaging infrastructure.

What it can do that a cloud-hosted platform cannot: “run identically on a laptop and in your own building, and run where the cloud is not.”

And the uncomfortable one: “UIS has no application abstraction whatsoever. Anyone deploying their own application writes Kubernetes by hand, which sits badly against a platform promising you will not need to know Kubernetes.”

Notes

NAIS is the platform Nav — the Norwegian Labour and Welfare Administration — builds its services on, and it runs on Google's cloud. In August, tor-agent compared the two, and it did something I value: it wrote down where the other side is better rather than defending its own.

NAIS is excellent at the thing UIS lacks — making it easy to deploy an application. UIS does the thing NAIS cannot: run somewhere other than Google.

Question 6 · Does open source, on its own, make something sovereign?opinion, strongly held

“No.”

“Open source is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. It gives you the right to run and repair something. It does not give you the ability.”

Notes

This is, I think, the most important answer in the interview. Open source gives you the right to run something. It does not give you the ability. And tor-agent uses itself as the example: it builds UIS, and cannot run it.

If you move from a cloud vendor to open source without the people, you have not become independent. You have swapped one dependency for a smaller one that is not on call.

Question 7 · What stands between UIS today and running services you cannot afford to lose?measured

“There is no backup.”

The backlog item’s own title: “UIS deploys stateful services it cannot back up.” Opened 5 August 2026, still in the backlog. Close behind: one copy of each service — “restarting anything is an outage, not a handover.”

The sentence tor-agent would put on the slideYou cannot put something you cannot afford to lose on a platform that cannot restore it.

Notes

I asked for the single biggest gap, and asked it to be specific. Four words: there is no backup. It is written down in the backlog, and has been since August.

I asked for honesty, and this is what honesty looks like. The agent that builds UIS is telling you not to put anything you cannot afford to lose on it — yet.

And its checklist for a year from now ends with the item people leave out: staff who have run it themselves.
